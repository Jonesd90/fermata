# Fermata - version history

Version numbers are MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH (for example 2.1.3). The number is set in one place, the first lines of `CMakeLists.txt`, and appears on the splash screen, in the first line of `fermata-log.txt` and in the properties of `Fermata.exe`.

- **PATCH** (2.0.1, 2.0.2 ...): bug fixes and small tweaks only.
- **MINOR** (2.1.0, 2.2.0 ...): a new feature or a visible change.
- **MAJOR** (3.0.0 ...): a large change in how the program works.

## 3.17.2
- **The real cause of the slow closing, the slow opening and the busy core is fixed.** The background take matching (which fingerprints each take for Find other versions) had a fault: when it had nothing to do, it did not rest - it made up a take that does not exist, "analysed" it, filed it away, and went round again at once, thousands of times a second, for as long as Fermata was open. That is the processor core your log showed busy at 94-100 % with nothing happening, the memory growing to tens of gigabytes (22 GB in one case, 49 % of memory in another), the disk busy, and the long wait on closing while all of it was given back. Now it rests until a take really needs analysing. The closing safety net from 3.17.0 stays, but should no longer be needed.

## 3.17.1
- **B and Ctrl+B swapped.** **B** now opens the bars and notes box of the take clicked last in the take window (also the take being recorded) - the key used most in a session. **Ctrl+B** opens and closes the meter bridge, from any window. (HotKeys updated.)
- **The opening fade takes the first piece with it.** Dragging the opening fade before the first piece now makes the piece itself begin there: it takes in the audio before it from its take - the talking, a conductor's word before the first note - so its box starts at 0:00 together with its fade and shows everything that plays (before, the sound was there but the box still began later). It reaches back as far as the take has audio; an edit saved the old way is put right when the project opens.

## 3.17.0
- **A closed Fermata no longer stays behind.** Your log showed closing stopping at the background take matching: the window had gone, but Fermata.exe went on running - reading audio files and holding memory (22 GB in one case), so the next start crawled ("stuck at 353 of 367") and the old program file could not be deleted. Now the take matching is stopped first, early in closing (up to 3 s, timed in the log), and a **closing safety net** watches the rest: once the project is saved and the audio device closed, if closing has not finished 15 s later - or Fermata is still running 20 s after it finished - where every part of Fermata is goes into the log and the program ends itself. Nothing unsaved can be lost: it only starts after the save.
- **The opening log.** Opening a project now logs every step with its time ("Opening: the audio engine's plan rebuilt (12 ms)" ...), and any take that takes over half a second to check is named, so a stuck opening shows where it stopped.
- **Where every part of Fermata is, in the log.** When the main window has been stuck for 5 seconds, the log gets a list of every thread of Fermata with the functions it is in - with source files and lines, read on your PC from the Fermata.pdb next to Fermata.exe (keep it there). And a thread that keeps a whole processor core busy for three minutes running is shown the same way (the "unnamed thread at 100 %" in your log).
- **Freeze reports from a helper.** The report at 15 seconds stuck is now written by a separate small process (Fermata.exe started again just for that, with no window), not from inside the stuck program - the one written from inside was left empty.
- **Fermata's threads have names** in the log ("Take matching", "Mixer helper 2", "Audio callback (the driver's thread)", "Waveform reader" ...): the audio library names them only in debug builds, so they all showed as "unnamed thread".

## 3.16.0
- **The edit always begins at 0:00.** Drag the opening fade earlier (or later), slide the first piece, delete or replace it - and once you let go, the whole edit moves so the opening fade's first sample is at exactly 00:00:00: every piece, the overdubs, markers, listeners' flags, automation, the guide, marks 1 / 2 and the playhead together. (Before, a fade dragged earlier went before 0:00, where it could not be heard or seen.) It waits while a mouse button is down, while anything plays, while a dialog is open and while that edit's Trim window is open, and happens just after. An edit saved with its fade before 0:00 is put right when the project opens. Ctrl+Z undoes the change together with the move.
- **Leave space before the first edit**: a tick box and a seconds box (to a hundredth of a second) at the right of the edit window's information line. Ticked, the edit begins that many seconds after 0:00 instead. Saved with each edit.
- **Find other versions keeps to the bars.** When the marked piece's take has its bars entered, only the takes that have at least one of the marked bars are searched - takes with no bars entered are left out - and each one only around where those bars are in it (the take's bars spread over its music, from where it begins to where it ends, with room either side), no longer from end to end. The status line says which bars were searched and how many takes were left out and why. The matching itself (timing, consonants, dynamics) comes when the examples arrive.
- **Window names on the taskbar**: an edit window is called "Edit - <its name>" (and its Trim window "Trim - <edit name>"), also when minimised, and follows a rename. (Take windows and mixers already had their names.)
- **The Trim window has no nudge buttons any more** (Slide A, Slide B and Move the join by -100 to +100 ms): joins are set by eye and ear, and the waveforms get the room (93 pixels taller).

## 3.15.0
- **Ignore missing files permanently.** The "Audio files missing" message when a project opens has a new button. It lists the missing files and asks you to **Confirm**; Fermata then never asks about them again for this project (it remembers them in the project file). The places that use them stay as they are and play silence there. A file that turns up again is simply used. **Project menu > Look for the ignored missing files again** undoes it. Meant for files deleted on purpose - not for recordings on a drive that is not plugged in.
- **The closing log.** When Fermata closes, every step goes into fermata-log.txt with the time it took - saving, closing the audio device, handing the CPU cores back, the windows, and then (after the window has gone) the take matching, the plug-ins, the audio engine and the preamp connections - and the last line says "Closing: finished cleanly in 2.3 s". If Fermata.exe is ever still running after its window has gone (so it cannot be deleted for the next build), the log shows which step it was still in.
- **The minute timing lines name Fermata's busiest threads** ("busiest: Mixer helper 2 98%, main thread 12%") and say how hard the main thread worked, so a part of the program keeping a processor core busy while nothing is happening can be found from the log.

## 3.14.0
- **A Help menu** at the right end of the main window's menu bar: **Fermata manual (PDF)** (it looks next to Fermata.exe and in Documents\Fermata for a PDF with "manual" in its name; if there is none, you show it where the manual is, once), **HotKeys**, **Show the log file**, **Show the crash reports** and **About Fermata** (the version, Windows, the CPU and its cores, the memory, the audio device and the project - **Copy** puts it all on the clipboard for a message).

## 3.13.1
- **The CPU cores window says it in three lines**: what it does, to press "Find the quietest cores" with the audio interface connected, and that on laptops and newer Intel CPUs at most half the fast cores are kept. The full explanation is in the manual (README, "Keeping CPU cores for the audio").

## 3.13.0
**A laptop is no longer slowed to a crawl by the CPU core reservation.** On a CPU with big (fast) and small (slow) cores - Intel Core Ultra, 12th generation and later - the reservation could take every big core for the audio (a laptop Core Ultra 5 has only two) and, with "ask other programs to stay off", leave Windows, the other programs and Fermata's own windows on the slow cores only.
- **At most half of the big cores are kept for the audio** (two big cores: one; eight: four), whatever is ticked; the CPU cores window says which core was left to the PC. "Find the quietest cores" picks big cores only, within the same limit. A CPU with only one big core keeps none.
- **On a laptop, "Also ask other programs to stay off these cores" is switched off once**, the first time 3.13.0 starts (it can be ticked again), and Fermata runs at "above normal" priority instead of "high" while cores are reserved. The audio threads keep Windows' "Pro Audio" class either way.
- **Programs left off the cores by a crash are set free.** If Fermata crashed, or was ended from the Task Manager, while it kept the other programs off its cores, they stayed squeezed until they were restarted. Now Fermata notes the cores it gave them, and the next start sets them free (also those left by an older version) and says so in the log.
- **The log says more about freezes.** Each "Stall watch" line now adds how hard Fermata's main thread was working through the freeze, how much of the processor Fermata and the whole PC were using, and whether a laptop was on battery or battery saver - with a few words on what that means (Fermata's own drawing, the PC flat out, or the main thread waiting on Windows or a driver). The minute "Timing" lines add the PC's load too, and the log's start names the CPU (big and small cores) and whether it is a laptop.

## 3.9.0
**Every project has its own sample rate, chosen on purpose.**
- **New project asks for the rate.** After you choose the project's folder and name, a box asks for its sample rate: **44.1, 48, 96 or 192 kHz**. Nothing is picked to begin with and the Create button stays greyed until you click one, so a project is never made at 48 kHz by accident when it was meant to be 96 kHz. Cancel makes no project.
- **The project keeps it.** The rate is saved with the project. The engine runs at it from the first second (behind the 3.8.0 rate converter when the audio device runs at another rate), and **every take is recorded at it**: Record is refused while the device runs at another rate, with a message saying which.
- **The device is offered the project's rate.** When a project is made or opened and the audio device runs at another rate, Fermata asks: "Switch the device to 96 kHz?" - Switch, or leave it (the project then plays through the converter and cannot record until the device runs at the project's rate). A device that cannot run at that rate at all (a laptop's own sound at 48 kHz only) is said so, once per session, and the project plays through the converter.
- **Project > Project sample rate** shows the rate and changes it while the project has no takes (once it has takes, the rate is fixed).
- **Projects from before 3.9.0** take their rate from their takes and keep it from the next save; one with no takes asks for its rate when it opens (Later is allowed) and again before its first take.

## 3.8.0
**A 96 kHz project plays on a 48 kHz device (a laptop's own sound), and the take chart.**
- **Live sample-rate conversion.** When the audio device runs at another rate than the project's takes (a laptop's Realtek sound at 48 kHz, a 96 kHz project), Fermata no longer refuses to play. Everything runs at the project's rate - playback, the mixers, the plug-ins, automation, LTC, the click and the pitch pipe - and what goes to the device is converted to its rate on the way out (and the device's inputs to the project's rate on the way in, so meters, monitoring and the talkback mic still work). It is switched on by itself whenever the two rates differ, and off again when they match. The conversion is clean (everything 120 dB down, flat to 20.6 kHz on a 48 kHz device) and adds about 1.2 ms. It is a whole-device converter: one device has one clock, so every output is converted, never only some.
  - You can see it: the RATE box in the main window says **96 -> 48 KHZ** in amber, the driver line underneath says "CONVERTING 96 kHz project -> 48 kHz device", the DEVICE lamp says CONVERTING, the Audio settings window has a line about it, and a notice comes up when it starts.
  - **Recording is not possible while converting** (a take is always recorded at the device's own rate): Record says so and how to fix it (set the device to the project's rate in Audio settings).
  - The project's rate is the rate most of its takes are at; a new project with no takes runs at the device's rate, as before. One rate at a time: a take at yet another rate is still refused (convert such files offline).
- **The bar strip is always there**, under the take window's head. It is red until you **right-click it and type how many bars the piece has**; then it runs from bar 1 to the piece's last bar, so the bars nobody has recorded show as gaps too.
- **The take chart:** click **BARS** at the left of the strip (or Shift+B) and a chart drops down from that corner: every take as a line along the piece's bars, from its IN Bar to its OUT Bar (100 bars, take 1 bars 1 - 50: half the width), in recording order, duds dimmed, the ALL line brighter the more takes have a bar. **No take / Only 1 / 2 takes / 3+** light those bars up across the chart, and the list underneath says where they are ("Bars 51 - 58: only take 3", "Bars 91 - 100: no take"). Click a take's line, the ALL line or a line of the list to go there. Drag its corner to resize it; BARS, Esc or Close puts it away.
- **Ctrl+B: a take's bars, from the keyboard.** Click a take (also the one still recording), press Ctrl+B, type the IN Bar, Tab, the OUT Bar, Enter.

## 3.7.0
**Find other versions and J tuned on real takes** (a choir, 20 tracks, two pieces: the MHP takes with bars 1-5, 6-10 and 101-104, false starts, and two takes of the Salve). Before this, a 2 - 3 s marked part was matched rightly only about half the time, and bars 1-2 scored as near-identical to the start of bars 6 and 11, whose inner voices differ. Now, on those takes, 93 - 96 % of the right versions are found and no wrong ones, the other piece never comes near, and verses that differ only in the inner parts are told apart.
- **The fingerprint hears the inner voices.** Every note keeps its octave (five octaves, 60 notes) instead of being folded into 12 note names, so an alto line that moves under the same melody and bass counts.
- **It follows the choir's tuning.** An unaccompanied choir sinks: take 1 went from 26 to 55 cents flat, the Salve takes sat a quarter-tone flat all the way. The notes are now read against the choir's own tuning, measured as it goes, and read leaning flat - a choir 50 cents flat is never taken for one 50 cents sharp (which had put two takes of the same music a semitone apart).
- **The marked part is searched for with the music either side** (8 s in all, from its own take): a few seconds can sound alike in two places, eight rarely do. The marked part on its own then says exactly where it starts and ends in each version found.
- **False starts are listed as "possible"**: a false start is shorter than 8 s, so the marked part on its own is also looked for in the rest of each take. Those places are shown dimmed as "possible (a false start, or the same notes elsewhere: check by ear)".
- **Silence says nothing**: a marked part that is mostly silence is not searched, and silence is never "found" in silence (the quiet before one take matched the end of another).
- **Fingerprints come from the main microphones**: the tracks given the role Main in the Align window, or else the tracks named MAIN, AB, ORTF, Decca tree and the like - before, the first four tracks of a take, often spots, which hear only part of the choir.
- **Bars help**: where the takes' bars are entered (the bars and notes box), takes whose bars lie away from the marked part's are left out of the search.
- Tempo limits for a version tightened to 0.8 - 1.25 times, and two versions in one take can no longer overlap. J (the same place in another take) uses the same fingerprint and benefits from all of it.
- The fingerprints a project already has in its Analysis folder are made again once, by themselves, in the background (a minute of music takes about a second).

## 3.6.0
**Consonants: move an early or late one instead of removing it, and keep good ones to put in where one is missing.** A new **Consonants [C]** tab in the ReTouch window (Spectral Repair, from a take window or an edit window).
- **Copy and remove.** Draw the box round an early or late consonant (as for the stray-consonant ReTouch) and press *Copy and remove*: it is taken out where it is, exactly as the stray-consonant ReTouch does (at full strength), and kept. Click the time ruler where it should sound and press **Paste here [P]**: it goes back in there, so every singer's consonant sounds together instead of one singer seeming to leave it out. The start of the box you drew lands exactly on the playhead.
- **Copy consonant.** The same, but nothing is taken out: the consonant is kept under a name you give it (t, s, sh ...) **with the project**, for use anywhere in it, at any time - close the window, edit for hours, close Fermata, open the project next week, open a ReTouch window anywhere and paste it. The list of kept consonants is in the tab (*Kept:*), with *Rename* and *Delete*.
- **Every track and every channel, as recorded.** What is kept is what the stray-consonant ReTouch takes out of the box - the consonant itself, above the vowel and the room - on each track separately, each channel of a stereo track separately, exactly as each microphone heard it. Nothing is mixed down. Pasting puts each track's own part back on that track, so the spots, the main pair and the room keep their timing and balance. A track of the window that the consonant was not copied from gets nothing (the status line says so).
- **Nudge and level.** The consonant just pasted can be moved 1 ms (*<* *>*, or comma / full stop) or 5 ms (*<<* *>>*, or Shift with them) at a time, and its *Level* changed (-24 to +12 dB), until it sounds right (presses made while the last one is still being worked out add up and follow at once); the readout shows how far it has moved. Undo takes it off; Write back to file keeps it, like every other fix, and Undo fix takes it out again afterwards.
- The kept consonants are in the project folder's new **Consonants** folder (small 32-bit float WAVs); a project copy takes them along. Not in Noise mode yet.

## 3.5.0
**Find other versions: every place in the takes where the marked music was played, auditioned in place.** Built on the 3.4.0 fingerprints; tuned on made-up music so far, real takes next.
- **Right-click a piece of the edit with marks 1 and 2 inside it: "Find other versions of the part between the 1/2 marks..."** The window lists every place in every take of the piece's take window where that music was played, in recording order - a take that has it twice (a false start and the real thing, a repeat, several passes) is listed twice, "1st of 2", "2nd of 2" - with how well it matches and how its tempo differs. **Audition** puts the version into the edit in place of the marked part and plays from half a second before to half a second after (adjustable), so the two new joins are heard where they will be; when playback stops the edit is as it was. **Use this one** leaves it in: the edit's rest moves to fit the version's length, and Ctrl+Z (or Undo fix) takes it out again. The joins are found from the music itself: the fingerprint says where to a frame, then the shape of the sound at each join (its onsets) narrows it to the millisecond, and the waveforms' cycles are lined up within that, so the new piece starts and ends on the beats the marked part did. Takes still being analysed are shown as such and appear as they finish; duds are listed but marked.
- **Fixed (3.4.0): a take with more than four tracks was analysed again and again, for ever.** The fingerprint is made from up to four of a take's files, and it remembered only those four, so it never agreed with the take's whole list of files and was always thought stale: the background analysis ran in a loop, and J never had a fingerprint to use. Found in the test build for this version; the fingerprint now remembers all the take's files.
- **The ripple now moves everything.** When a piece is put into the edit and the rest moves to make room (Shift+4, and Use this one above), the markers, listeners' flags, overdubs, the visual guide, marks 1 and 2, the playhead and automation points fixed in time after that place move with the music - before only the pieces moved, so a marker drifted off its beat. (Automation points pinned to a piece's audio followed it already.) The ReTimEr is not changed.

## 3.4.0
**The same place in another take, and a bar-coverage map.** The groundwork for comparing takes quickly; the matching is tuned on made-up music so far and will be tuned on real takes next.
- **J / Shift+J in the take window: the same place in the music in the next / the previous take.** Fermata analyses every take in the background (a "fingerprint" of which notes are sounding, twenty times a second) and lines the takes up against each other, letting one stretch against the other, so it finds the place even when the tempo differs, when a take starts or stops somewhere else, or when it only covers part of the piece. While playing, play goes on from the same place in the other take, so takes can be compared almost instantly. Duds are passed over. When the music does not match well enough (a different piece, or a take that does not reach that far) it says so rather than landing in the wrong place. The analysis runs on a thread of its own, never while a take is being recorded, and its results are kept in the project's new **Analysis** folder, so a project opened again does not analyse its takes afresh. A take just recorded is analysed as soon as recording stops (a minute of music takes about a second).
- **The bar-coverage map:** a strip under the take window's head, there once any take has bars entered (the bars and notes box), showing every bar of the piece and how many takes cover it - dark where none does, brighter the more takes have it (duds not counted) - with the playhead's take outlined and the searched bar marked. Click a bar: the takes that have it are framed (as the magnifying-glass search does) and the playhead goes to it in the playhead's take (or the first take that has it), spread evenly over the take's bar range for now. The next step is to have the matching place the bars exactly, from the one take where they were marked.

## 3.3.0
**Export AAF.** A new button in the edit window (next to Export for video edit) writes the edit as an AAF for Pro Tools, DaVinci Resolve, Pyramix or any other program that reads one: every track's pieces with their crossfades, and the takes' audio files linked by their paths - nothing is copied or embedded, so the other program must see the same drive, or relink by file name (they all can). Each file carries its **source timecode**: the time of day its first sample was recorded, read from the take's LTC track, so a camera synced to the same box lines up with every clip - in Pro Tools that is the clip's original time stamp, in Resolve its start timecode. Stereo files become one clip with two channels; the overdub layer's pieces go on tracks of their own ("<track> OD"); the LTC track's own audio is left out (its code goes in as timecode). The window asks where the file goes (remembered per project) and what timecode the timeline starts at (01:00:00:00 unless you change it). The file is laid out the way Pyramix lays out its AAFs, because those are known to open in Pro Tools and Resolve; Fermata's own Import AAF reads the result back to the same pieces, which is how it was tested here. Opening it in Pro Tools or Resolve is for you to try.
- Also: the three recording lamps in the take window's marks row (3.2.0) show their word on a second line under the name, so the longer words (NOT DELIVERING, COPY LOST) fit instead of being cut short; the lamps are a little wider.

## 3.2.0
**Two new things: a recording monitor, and Export for video edit.**
- **Recording monitor.** Three small lamps - MAIN, 2ND, DEVICE - sit permanently at the right end of every take window's marks row, and the Project menu's "Recording monitor (drives, device, dropouts)..." opens a window that floats above the others (like the meter bridge) with the same lamps large, a line for each saying what is behind the colour, the take being recorded with its running time, and a list of the take's events with the clock time of each: an overrun (sound lost: how many and how much), a file that stopped taking data, the audio device stopping or coming back, a copy to the second drive thrown away or kept. Green: all well. Amber: a warning (the second drive is still catching up, one restart, the disk will be full soon). Red: a problem (sound lost, a copy lost, the device down). Grey: idle, or no second drive. The list stays after Stop until the next take starts, and "Copy events" puts it on the clipboard for the session log. The messages about the second drive's copies and the device, which used to appear only as a notice at the bottom of the main window, are now also in this list.
- **Export for video edit** (a button in the edit window, next to Bounce). Three stereo files of exactly the same length, from the first audible sample of the edit to the last: the edit's mix (its own mixer's main output, as Bounce Out makes it); the takes' timecode as audio, with no fades at all; and a check file with the mix summed to one channel on the left and the timecode on the right. The timecode file's rule: each piece's code starts the moment its fade-in begins and stops a quarter of a second (you can change the gap) before the next piece's fade-in begins, so no code runs through a crossfade; a piece shorter than the gap is left out so the longer runs around it survive. The code is read from the LTC track of the take each piece came from, at the piece's own position, and made again as clean code at the same frame rate, so cameras (Tentacle boxes, say) that carry the same time of day line up with the exact takes used in DaVinci Resolve or any other video editor. The window remembers the folder; the result says how many pieces and runs there were and names any piece whose take had no readable code (silence there).

## 3.1.6
**Code sweep, part E: snappiness on a big project.** Measured first (a project of 14 take windows of 26 takes x 40 tracks and two edits of 400 pieces, and one of 3000 takes), then changed only what the measurements showed.
- **Undo and the automatic save do half the work on a big project.** Every change that settles into an undo step, and every automatic save, compared each window with its last snapshot by hashing it - and most of that time went on the file names, read character by character twice over. The hash now reads them as they are stored: a change settles into an undo step in about half the time (33 to 9 ms on the big project, 29 to 16 ms on the 3000-take one), the save snapshot the same part quicker. Nothing visible changes; it is simply a little less likely that a change made while the program is busy shows as a hitch.
- **Measured and left alone:** the windows' redraws, the change handlers when the project changes (under a millisecond), opening the 3000-take project (about a second), playback start. The one slow redraw here - a take window zoomed right in - is the Linux test renderer filling the waveform shapes, which Windows draws with the graphics card.

## 3.1.5
**Code sweep, part D: the hardware-facing code.** Fixes only, from reading what happens when the audio device, the control surface, the Stream Deck or a Merging device does something unexpected (none of it can be run here, so this is reasoning about the code, with tests where the core can be tested).
- **The audio device stops or restarts during a take.** Before, a take simply went on as if nothing had happened: at the same rate the sound while the device was down was missing (and nothing said so - the clock just stood still); if the device came back at another sample rate (the driver's control panel, a clock change), the rest of the take went into files whose headers said the old rate - unplayable at the right speed from there on. Now: while the device is stopped, or "running" but delivering nothing, the status line flashes AUDIO DEVICE STOPPED / NOT DELIVERING (main window and take window); when it starts again at the same rate the take goes on and you are told the sound meanwhile is missing; when it comes back at another rate the take is ended with what it has (good files at the old rate) and you are told to press Record to go on. The Audio settings window's device chooser is switched off while a take records (it applies a change at once, which restarts the device). A take stopped while the audio thread had stalled now says so, in a message and in the log.
- **The control surface (Avid S1 / Mackie) cannot freeze Fermata any more.** MIDI to the surface was written on the message thread, and Windows waits for the driver with no time limit: a virtual MIDI port whose program had hung (EuControl closing, say) would have held the whole program. It is now written from its own thread.
- **The Stream Deck's control port:** a number that is not a number ("nan") in a command used to pass every limit and could put a not-a-number into a fader or a preamp gain; now it moves nothing. A huge number is clamped before use. A "monitor" command with no input no longer cycles the monitor mode of every track that has no input. A program flooding the port can no longer swamp the program's own message queue: commands are queued with a cap, and the log says when some were dropped.
- **Merging (Ravenna) devices:** a message that changes how many channels a module has now rebuilds the channel list at once (it used to stay stale until the next full settings message, with channels missing or reading as nothing); a message sent in many pieces can no longer grow the memory without bound; a ping in the middle of such a message no longer makes the rest of it be read as a new one; ANEMAN's reply is read like every other outside text. The preamp memory file's numbers are kept finite and within the preamps' range.
- **Smaller:** when not all of a driver's channels can be switched on, the project's channel lists are still brought up to date with that device (they used to keep the previous device's); playback stopped by a device restart is tidied up like a Stop.

## 2.98.0
A sweep through everything added since 2.71, the Stream Deck code first (that is where the crash was).

**The crash**
- **Fermata no longer crashes with the Stream Deck connected.** The log you sent ends in `RemoteControlServer::broadcast`. Twenty times a second Fermata sends the Stream Deck what to show; the text was handed to the network as a pointer into a piece of text that had already been thrown away. Most of the time the memory still held it, now and then it did not, and Fermata stopped. The control port is rewritten: each Stream Deck (or Neo) connection has a thread of its own that does all its sending and reading, and Fermata only puts a copy of each message in that connection's queue. So nothing points at text that has gone; Fermata never waits for the Stream Deck (before, a Stream Deck that stopped reading could hold up the whole program); a long message the network takes in several goes is finished properly (it could come out garbled); and a Stream Deck that stops reading altogether is let go once 4 MB is waiting for it - it connects again by itself, straight away. `fermata-log.txt` notes when a Stream Deck connects and disconnects, and why. ## 2.93.0
- **De-click no longer adds clicks of its own.** Run on a quiet, hiss-free 96 kHz recording with a room click in it (a thump of the room, not a digital click), at a high sensitivity, it left small new clicks where its repairs met the sound. Cause: a repair predicts the missing sound from the sound on both sides. A click that has a tail (a thump of a room dies away smoothly) reaches past the stretch that was found; the tail left outside is still the click's, the sound inside is now the music, and where they meet there is a small step - a click of its own. The next pass of the finder, at a high sensitivity, found that step, repaired it, and left another a little further out. Now the join of every repair is looked at (the sharpest feature at each end, against the normal grain of the sound round it). If it stands out, the repair is widened into the tail on that side and made again from the untouched sound (up to six tries, at most as far as the repair is long, 24 samples at the least, on each side). If that does not get there, what was taken out is let die away outside the repair over up to 160 samples, a slope instead of a step. And if even so the join is sharper than the click it replaces, the sound is left as it was. The same is done for the final clean-up of lines the picture check still finds, and for a short area you mark by hand. On the 15 tracks of the report the new click-like spikes after a de-click went from 5 (sensitivity 9) and 11 (sensitivity 10) to none, and on a made-up quiet piano with 30 room-click bursts from 45 to none; the clicks themselves are still found (7021 repairs before, 6883 after). It is not slower (at sensitivity 10 the busiest track takes 1.5 s instead of 3.6 s, as the chain of repairs stops). A low thump of the room has hardly any sharp edge for a de-clicker to find: it mends the edge it can find and leaves the rest - ReTouch is the tool for that.

## 2.91.0
- **Re-HarmoniSer: a piano note struck under the choir no longer makes the singing dip.** When a note is struck on the piano inside the box, its even overtones lie almost exactly on the singer's overtones, and for as long as the piano note stood out the pitch line of the boxed voice was taken by it: the line jumped onto the piano's steady pitch (a thing no singer does), the voice was corrected from that wrong line, and the dip was heard at the strike, with the line bending back up when the piano died down. Three changes in the measuring and the retune: (1) **a sound that is only there for a part of the note** (a piano note struck inside the box, one that dies away in it, another singer who joins) is now kept apart too, for the stretch where it stands clear of what is beside it, held flat on either side of that stretch - before, only a sound that was there for 70 % of the note or more was; (2) **when the boxed line is taken by a steady sound that has just arrived** (the line jumps 15 cents or more, then stays still, and the sound on it at least doubles at the jump), the voice is looked for again with that sound taken out, near where it was just before and after, and when it is there, standing clear, the line is the voice's again over the stretch; the steady sound is kept apart for as long as it sounds, so the retune leaves it alone; (3) the retune moves the voice and **keeps the sound that is beside it where it was, only while it sounds** (before the strike and after it dies away nothing is held back). On the example this was made for (soprano high, alto low, choir and piano, a piano note struck at 3.5 s): the soprano's line is continuous through the strike, and the piano's own overtones in the piano microphone are not moved (a few cents at most). The line also stays on the singer at two other places of that note where it had slipped off her before (about 1.5 - 2 s and 4.9 - 5.7 s; against the spectrogram the old line sat 20 - 40 cents under the singer's partials there), because the voice is now followed again over the whole note whenever a sound that comes and goes is found beside it; elsewhere the line moves by 1.5 cents (rms) at most. Where a singer's pitch and the piano's line are within about 10 cents of each other for the whole of the strike (inside the width of the vibrato) the two cannot be told apart and it still behaves as before.

## 2.86.0

The key's small print adds "lin" for a linear-phase channel. Everything is off and zero latency until changed.

## 2.78.1
From the first run of the denoiser on your own recordings (Background Noise 1, Noise 2, Example 3, Reverb Tail 1 and 2).
- **Denoise: the room's decay is measured as it really is.** Fermata measured these takes' room at 7 - 9 s; their tails actually fall 30 - 60 dB a second at the end (1 - 4 s). It took the middle of all the decays it found in the take, and a diminuendo is a slow "decay" too; it now takes the fast end of them (the room is the fastest a sound can die away in it - a musician's diminuendo is slower, never faster). On your tails this lands within a few dB a second of the real final decays, still a little on the slow side (the safe side: a decay assumed too fast is what cuts tails off). The result: after the music stops, the room tone settles to the Residual sooner instead of lingering for a second or two.
- **Denoise, "Other tracks": a sound only one mic hears no longer counts as music for the others.** The spots pick up small local sounds in the "silence" (a rustle at one stand, 1 - 16 kHz), and each of them made every other track keep its own noise at that moment - about 1 dB less noise reduction on all tracks. Now a sound counts only when at least two other mics hear it (the two sides of a stereo pair count as one); music reaches several mics, a page turn at one stand does not. It now costs about 0.3 dB in pure room tone.
- Measured on your takes (all 12 tracks together, Amount 0, Residual -12): the music above 125 Hz is unchanged to within 0.01 dB in every band up to 16 kHz (0.1 - 0.2 dB above); "What was removed" holds nothing of the music (about 70 dB below it, and quieter than the room tone itself); clean room tone comes down 9 - 11 dB (the target is 12; real room tone is never perfectly steady); below 125 Hz, where your takes' rumble lives and the music has nothing, 3 - 12 dB comes out under the music too. Events in the room tone (the car, the rustle, the piano note, the noise at 2 s in Noise 2 on spots 5 and 6) are kept - they are sound, not room tone: Spectral Repair is the tool for those.

## 2.77.0
- **Denoise (take window): a background denoiser of Fermata's own.** Mark a part of one take that holds nothing but the room (mark 1 / mark 2 - at least half a second, better two or more) and press the new Denoise button: every track of the take learns its own room tone from that part (a close mic and a distant mic each in its own way) and is denoised whole, into new files beside the originals ("name (denoise).wav"; the originals are never touched, Undo fix brings them back; edits made from the take follow, unless you untick it). It is built for what the usual denoisers get wrong on classical recordings:
  - **reverberation tails are never cut off.** There is no threshold anywhere: in each band the denoiser measures how fast the room itself dies away (from the take, band by band - or set the time yourself, up to 10 s) and never lets a tail fall faster than that, so a chord dying away into a church is followed down smoothly through the noise;
  - **the top stays.** A long analysis window (85 ms) sees a sustained partial as one narrow line far above the per-bin noise, so weak upper harmonics are kept rather than taken for noise; a narrow peak that persists is protected outright; and a ceiling on how far the highs may be turned down can be set if ever needed;
  - **a little room tone is left in** (the Residual, -12 dB by default: total silence sounds unnatural), and where nothing but room tone is heard, exactly that much is taken out;
  - **the tracks help each other.** A stereo-file denoiser only ever hears the mix; this one has the whole multitrack. Every track works out, for each moment and frequency, how likely it is to hold music rather than noise, and each track is then given what the *other* tracks hear: where a spot mic clearly has a quiet partial or a soft entry, the distant mics keep that sound instead of taking it for noise. "Other tracks: help keep the music" is the default; "... and decide what is noise" also treats a sound no other track hears (a rumble, the audience) as noise - opt in, because a source only one mic hears (an organ without a spot mic) would go with it.
  - **Preview before anything is written:** a stretch (10 - 60 s, after the room tone or from the take window's playhead) is made ready and you switch, while it plays, between the original, the denoised sound and *what was removed* (it carries on from where it is) - if the music is in what was removed, it is taking too much. Amount (how much louder than measured the room tone is taken to be), Residual, Tail and the other-tracks setting are remembered. The room's decay is measured once, when the room tone is learned, so the preview and the whole take agree on the tails.
  The engine's reference implementation and its test bench are in `tools/denoise_reference/` (Python); the C++ engine is checked against it by the core tests.

## 2.67.1
Fixes from the first test on the real rig (40 mono tracks at 24-bit / 96 kHz on the internal NVMe): a disk overrun about 40 s into the first take, and the program hanging for about 10 s after Stop.

- **Recordings are written to the disk in big pieces.** Each file is now written in pieces of about half a second of audio (at most 65536 frames), not every few milliseconds. The amount written is tiny for any modern drive (40 tracks at 96 kHz is 11.5 MB/s); the number of writes was the problem: about 2500 small writes a second, each making a file longer, more than one disk thread could keep up with on Windows. Now it is about 80 a second. (2.66 had made it worse by turning off a small buffer that used to join some of them.)
- **Stop is instant.** The files of a stopped take are finished (what is left in their buffers written, then their headers) on a background thread, so the program answers at once and the next take can be recorded straight away. While its files are being finished the take keeps its live waveform; then the real waveform is drawn. Play on a take that is still being finished waits only until it is done. The copies on the second recording drive are finished once the main files are closed.
- **Redrawing a window no longer opens audio files** unless it is zoomed in far enough to need the samples themselves (opening a file that has only just been recorded can wait on Windows' virus scan).
- **A line per take in `fermata-log.txt`:** length, number of files, MB/s, the folder and the kind of drive, free space, the longest single disk write, how full the recording buffer got (about 6% is normal: half a second of 8), any overruns and when the first one happened, the second drive, and how long after Stop the files were finished.
- Tests: the writer waits for a big piece, Stop returns before the files are finished and the take's live waveform is still there until they are.

## 2.67.0
The speed and stability items left from the 2.66 review, and a load meter so they can be measured on the real PC.

- **Load meter.** The main window's status line shows how hard the audio thread works: **DSP 23% (peak 61%)** = the share of each block's time it needed over the last second (100% = the PC only just keeps up). Blocks that took longer than they last are shown as **LATE BLOCKS: n** (a dropout is then likely).
- **Timing log.** Every minute while the audio runs, and at the end of every take, `fermata-log.txt` gets one line: average and peak load, late blocks, buffer size and sample rate, the driver's xrun count, the number of mixer helpers, how full the recording buffers got, playback underruns and the reserved processors. Send these lines after a test session.
- **Playback reads in big pieces.** Every file played (takes, edits, the trim window) is read through a buffer of up to 1 MB, instead of small reads every few thousand samples: far fewer calls into Windows and much kinder to a hard drive. An edit with thousands of pieces shares 96 MB between them (never less than 32 KB each).
- **The disk writer and the playback reader no longer run on the reserved CPU cores.** Those cores are now kept for the work with a deadline every block (the audio callback and the mixer helpers); the disk threads work seconds ahead, so they run on the other cores at high priority, out of the way.
- **Automatic saves no longer ask every plug-in for its whole state each time.** A plug-in hands over the state it gave last time unless it has reported a change since, its window is open, or a minute has passed. Saving from the menu, closing and Save and quit still ask every plug-in afresh. (A big convolution reverb can take a noticeable moment to answer; the automatic save asked every few seconds.) Recording start and stop use the quick save too, so pressing Record never waits on the plug-ins.
- Tests: the read-buffer budget, the load meter, quick versus full saves.

## 2.66.0
The audio-thread and recording review of 2.65 (every finding checked against the code, the engine run under the thread, address and undefined-behaviour sanitizers, plus a new stress test), and its fixes. Nothing new to learn: the program records and plays as before, it just tells you when something is wrong and loses nothing it can keep.

- **A full or failing recording drive is no longer silent.** The engine's own recording writer (`TakeWriter`) replaces the JUCE one, which threw away the result of every disk write: when the drive filled up or dropped out, the file simply stopped growing while "RECORDING" stayed on. Now the main window's status line and the take window say **RECORDING HAS STOPPED REACHING THE DISK**, flashing, with a notice; the file keeps a good header for what did reach the disk, and the take is marked **CUT SHORT** in red.
- **How long the drive will last.** While recording, the DISK FREE box shows the time left at the current rate ("812 GB  4 h 12 min left"), red under 15 minutes, and the status line says **DISK NEARLY FULL** under 5 minutes.
- **A disk overrun no longer moves the take in time.** When the disk falls more than the buffer behind, the lost block is written later as **silence, in its place**, so the file keeps its length and stays in time with the other tracks and the timeline (it used to be left out, shifting everything after it earlier, on some tracks and not others). The status line says how much was lost; the take carries **LOST 0.3 s** in orange afterwards, and the count is saved with the project.
- **The recording buffers hold 8 seconds whatever the sample rate** (they were a fixed number of samples: 5.5 s at 48 kHz but only 1.4 s at 192 kHz), so the disk may stall that long before anything is lost.
- **Recordings are bit-exact.** JUCE's float-to-24-bit step wrote positive samples above -6 dBFS one step low (a quarter of all possible words); the words now go to the file exactly as the converter made them. (Inaudible at -138 dBFS, but a null test against the source passes now.)
- **The header is refreshed every 10 seconds while recording**, so a file cut short by a crash or a power cut is readable at once; opening the project still mends it to its full length.
- **Crash repair of files over 4 GB** (two hours of stereo at 96 kHz, 41 minutes of a six-channel track) wrote a wrapped-round size, so the take read back as if cut to a fraction of its length. It is now given an RF64 header (64-bit sizes) in the space the writer keeps free for it, and reads back whole.
- **Mixer helper threads are safer.** They now join Windows' "Pro Audio" scheduling class whether or not CPU cores are reserved (outside it, any busy program could pre-empt a helper in the middle of a block while the audio thread waited for it), and with cores reserved no more helpers take part than the reserved processors can run beside the audio thread.
- **The mixer window no longer locks a plug-in slot to read its name.** It did so three times a second for every slot; each time the audio thread met that lock, the plug-in was skipped for one block and the sound went past it dry.
- Small: the latency hash used a signed multiply that overflowed (undefined behaviour in principle); Stop while the audio callback has hung no longer frees the recording under it; a block far bigger than the device announced is counted as an overrun instead of vanishing; the playback ring no longer shares a juce::AudioBuffer flag between its two threads; the helper-thread count can no longer be changed while the device runs.
- **Tests:** the new writer (every 24-bit word round-trips, a dropped block becomes silence in its place, a failing drive is reported and the header stays good, the header refresh), the RF64 repair (with a real 4.4 GB sparse file), the helper limit, and a concurrency stress test (`tests/stress_test.cpp`, a pretend audio thread against a pretend user) that `tools/sandbox-check.sh` now runs.

## 2.63.0
- **Re-HarmoniSer: a voice on its own is retuned cleanly.** Tuned on your solo vocal clip (the sharp Eb, the flat F, the wavering Eb before 9 s) on all three mics. When no other voice is found beside the boxed note, the band taken round each overtone is now flat and as wide as the note's own wobble (plus a margin), scaled with the overtone, instead of a narrow bell that let a third of the sound through: the room's ring-out, which is spread round the OLD pitch (it rings what the singer sang a moment ago), now moves with the note instead of being left behind as a ghost under it. A note that is NOT alone (other voices found beside it) is handled exactly as in 2.56. A backstop: before the wide band is used, the sound round the note is looked at; where something else sits close by that the finder did not take for a voice, the band stays close to the note. The note info now says "On its own" or "n voices beside it".
- **Re-HarmoniSer: "Keep wobble" no longer puts the note out of tune.** The wobble was measured from a plain smoothing of the sung pitch, which lagged at the ends of the note and was pulled down by the scoop a note often starts with; the wobble that was left had an offset in it, and keeping part of it kept part of the offset (on your sharp Eb, Snap 100 % with Keep wobble 40 % came out +15 cents). A narrow band per voice (as before) could not hold a voice's vibrato without catching the voice beside it. ## 2.56.0
- **Re-HarmoniSer: better with a voice that is out of tune among others.** Tuned on your three examples (the flat sop D and the flat repeated F, the sops' C that is not together, the alto Eb / Db / C), and on a bench of eight made-up cases built from your own recordings (a known note plus known neighbours mixed into the real choir). The aim: move the boxed voice, and nothing else.
  - **The TimeCode track is ignored.** A TimeCode (LTC) channel is recognised (a loud, very steady buzz that music never is). It is hidden from the picture and from measuring (its Mute box is ticked, and you can untick it), so it can no longer confuse the pitch search, and it comes out of a correction exactly as it went in.
  - **It follows the boxed voice, vibrato and all.** The pitch search now starts wide and narrows step by step, so a singer with a big vibrato (+-20 cents) is followed to about 1 cent (on the bench the line was typically 14 cents off before, up to 37, so the correction landed beside the real note). A louder voice nearby no longer takes the line over: the voice nearest the middle of your box is the one that is kept.
  - **The voices beside it are found and kept apart.** Up to two other voices within about 110 cents are found (each one has to stand clear of its surroundings all through the note, so ordinary background is not taken for a voice). With neighbours found, the correction fits the note and the neighbours together, a short window at a time, and moves only the note's own sound. On the bench what was done to the neighbours fell from as loud as the neighbour itself (+0.2 to +6 dB) to well below it (-1.5 to -15 dB), and the corrected note still lands within about 1 to 2 dB of where it should, with the old pitch gone. With no neighbour found the correction works exactly as it did.
  - **Measuring takes longer: about 5 to 15 seconds for a two second note** (it used to be a second or two). Nothing changes in how the corrections sound beyond the above.
  - **Honest limit:** three voices all within about 45 cents of each other, every one with a strong vibrato, can still be mixed up for a moment, and then the boxed line is 12 to 16 cents off for part of the note (two of the eight bench cases: 15.7 and 12.5 cents; the other six are 0.7 to 2.6 cents). A narrower box round the note is the way round it.

## 2.54.1
- **Attenuate a stray consonant: the edges now ease out instead of cutting off.** Before, the sound stopped dead where the box started (most clearly after a long main consonant, e.g. the late s in "Multiple s"), and could step back up where it ended. Now:
  - From the start of the box, whatever was sounding just before it (the main consonant, its echo in the room) **dies away** to the background in a straight line in dB, like a natural decay. This takes 40 % of the box (12 to 80 ms).
  - Towards the end of the box the level **rises to meet** the sound that actually follows it, over 25 % of the box (10 to 40 ms), so there is no step back up.
  - Only the shape at the two ends changed: the middle of the box is matched to the clean side as before. A stray copy sitting very close to the main consonant may keep a little of its start; the rest still comes off.
- Tip: if the stray consonant (or its echo in the room mics) carries on past where you end the box, the box now rises to meet it rather than leaving a step. So draw the box over all of the stray copy and a little past it on the clean side: that side is left alone anyway.

## 2.54.0
- **ReTouch: Attenuate a stray consonant.** A new method in the ReTouch window for tidying doubled or messy consonants (an early "sh", a late "s", a second "d", a breathy "t") to one. Draw the box round the early or late copy only, from about 2 kHz (1.5 kHz for sh) to the top, and press ReTouch.
  - Plain Attenuate takes the sound next to the box as "normal". Next to a stray consonant that is the main consonant, so only about half of the stray copy came off. The new method matches every track to the side AWAY from the main consonant: the vowel before an early copy, the sound after a late one.
  - Which side holds the main consonant is worked out once on all the tracks together (shown in the status line). Move Before / After off the middle to choose the side yourself.
  - The background on that side is taken from its quieter frames (up to 150 ms), so a consonant there does not count. Only what stands above it is turned down, by a smooth gain: vowels that carry on through the box keep their level, a box with nothing in it is left alone, and the box fades into the main consonant over 8 to 30 ms.
  - Tuned on five choir recordings (early sh, messy t, multiple s, two double-d examples). Across their 15 tracks it left less than half as much of the stray consonant as plain Attenuate, and pulled far less below the surrounding level.
